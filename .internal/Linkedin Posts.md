## Post A: Unifying Standards with an Upper-Level Ontology

How do we make HL7 FHIR, OMOP, or openEHR finally speak the same language—*without* creating "yet another standard"? Health-RI's answer: build a unifying **upper-level ontology** as a semantic bridge.
Our **Health-RI Semantic Interoperability Initiative** defines the precise meaning of key concepts across widely used health data standards and models【1】.
Instead of maintaining N×N pairwise mappings among standards, each standard maps *once* to a shared ontology【2】.
The result? If system A and system B both align to the ontology, they can understand each other's data with far less effort and ambiguity.
Crucially, we're **not replacing** existing standards that institutions invested in – we're complementing them.
This "semantic backbone" is non-intrusive, reducing ad-hoc translation layers【2】 while preserving each standard's context.
It's ambitious, but early feedback from experts in academia and industry is guiding us and keeping us aligned with national/international interoperability efforts【1】.
All components – the ontology, mapping files, etc. – are openly available.
**Call to action:** *Explore our documentation*【1】 and join the conversation.
By collaborating on mappings or use-cases, you can help ensure health data truly fulfills the "I" in FAIR (Interoperable).
Together, we can turn this vision into reality.

## Post B: Model-Driven Ontology – Bridging Conceptual and Technical

Ever read an ontology and wondered what the classes *really* mean? We did too – so we took a "model-first" approach.
Before writing a single OWL axiom, we crafted an **OntoUML** model of our domain. (OntoUML is a conceptual modeling language grounded in the Unified Foundational Ontology, UFO.)
This abstract model captures healthcare concepts in human terms.
Then we implemented those same concepts in OWL using **gUFO** – a lightweight OWL version of that foundational ontology【3】.
In other words, OntoUML provides the semantic blueprint at the **Computation-Independent Model (CIM)** level, and gUFO translates it into a machine-readable **Platform-Independent Model (PIM)** ontology【3】.
Why go to this extra effort? Because it **ensures clarity** about meaning vs. implementation.
Every class in our OWL file has an explicit definition in the OntoUML diagram – not just an arbitrary label【4】.
For example, we define a "Person" concept with OntoUML (with all its real-world nuances) before encoding it as `hrio:Person` in OWL.
This two-layer approach improves trust and consistency: domain experts can validate the conceptual model, while developers and AI systems work with the formal ontology, and the two remain aligned【4】.
It's knowledge engineering best-practice, brought into health data semantics.
**Call to action:** *Review our modeling guidelines* and share your feedback.
If you're an ontologist or data modeler, we welcome your expertise to refine our ontology – let's make sure our models resonate with real-world healthcare contexts.

## Post C: Mapping the Landscape – Vocabulary and Alignment Sets

Tired of writing custom data mappings between every system? So are we.
That's why Health-RI created an **open mapping layer** to connect local standards to our reference ontology.
A core deliverable is our **SSSOM mapping set** – a machine-readable file of mappings in the *Simple Standard for Sharing Ontology Mappings (SSSOM)* format【5】.
It's curated by our team **and** external collaborators, and we version it with full history (so no mapping is lost, only superseded).
This mapping set uses a custom **mapping vocabulary** we developed to precisely describe alignments【6】.
For example, we introduce properties like `hriv:hasExactMeaning` to link a source concept to a reference concept when they represent the same real-world meaning【6】.
In practice, this lets us assert, say, **FHIR's** `Patient` class has the exact same intended meaning as our ontology's `Person` class【7】.
By capturing mappings in a standardized way, we avoid hard-coding translations in each application.
Organizations can even embed these mappings in their own ontologies or maintain them externally (non-invasively) – either way, the semantics are clear.
The payoff is huge: once a mapping from Standard X to the reference ontology is done, you don't need to re-do it for Standard Y to X, Z to X, and so on.
All mappings converge on the hub.
**Call to action:** *Check out our mapping specs*【6】.
If your team maintains a healthcare data model or terminology, consider contributing a mapping!
We have guidelines and even a submission checklist to help you get started.
By pooling our mappings, we build a **community-driven interoperability catalog** that benefits everyone.

## Post G: Reproducible releases, append-only history

Interoperability fails when you can't tell **which** ontology or mapping version a result used.
Health-RI's publishing model treats ontology and SSSOM mappings as **releasable, citable artifacts** with dated versions (`YYYY-MM-DD`) and **append-only** supersession chains.
Value: every row in the mapping set remains auditable; fixes don't overwrite history—they **supersede** via `replaces`.
Ontology versions carry a version IRI and changelog.
Artifacts: **Publications** policy and **Mapping Governance** (lifecycle states; validation; promotion gates).
Impact: deterministic reruns, clean provenance, easy rollbacks, and trust for regulators/funders.
**Call to action:** If you operate data services or analytics, record both the ontology **version IRI** and mapping **release date** in your pipelines.
Refs: [Publications][9]; [Mapping governance: versioning & promotion gates][7]. (Accessed 6 Nov 2025)

## Post H: Exact meaning vs broader/narrower: mapping with intent

Not all "matches" are equal.
Health-RI's **Mapping Vocabulary (hriv)** distinguishes **`hasExactMeaning`**, **`hasBroaderMeaningThan`**, and **`hasNarrowerMeaningThan`** and aligns them to SKOS for pragmatic tooling.
Value: you capture **intended meaning** (intentional semantics) while still deriving SKOS matches for interoperability.
Plus, policy enforces **one** `hasExactMeaning` per external concept—removing ambiguity.
Artifacts: **Mapping Vocabulary** spec (v1.1.0) and **Mapping Strategy** with visual examples and SKOS entailments.
Impact: cleaner integrations, fewer silent misclassifications, and explainable mappings during reviews.
**Call to action:** When curating alignments, pick the **hriv** relation first on meaning grounds; let SKOS fall out via entailment.
See examples and predicate rules in the spec.
Refs: [Mapping vocabulary][6]; [Mapping strategy][4]. (Accessed 6 Nov 2025)

## Post I: How to contribute: issue form, template, and the two-person rule

Quality mappings need disciplined intake.
Health-RI supports two routes: a **GitHub issue form** (single-row SSSOM) and an **XLSX template** (batch rows + CURIE prefixes).
The mapping schema requires **language-tagged labels** (e.g., `Patient@en`, `Patiënt@nl`) and provenance (author/reviewer ORCIDs).
Governance enforces **separation of duties**: author ≠ reviewer; curator publishes after validation.
Releases are dated; history is append-only.
Artifacts: **Mapping Set schema** (fields, defaults, stable w3id URIs) and **Governance** (roles, lifecycle, checks).
Impact: trustworthy mappings others can reuse with confidence.
**Call to action:** Propose a mapping via the issue form or XLSX; include labels with language tags and rationale for non-obvious choices.

## Post K: Why CIM→PIM (OntoUML→gUFO) improves alignment

We separate **meaning** from **implementation**.
First, a **CIM** (Computation-Independent Model) in **OntoUML** captures domain semantics humans can vet; then we implement a **PIM** (Platform-Independent Model) in OWL using **gUFO**.
Value: every OWL class has an **explicit conceptual anchor**, reducing "label-only" modeling and drift.
The strategy clarifies where semantics live and how external ontologies map to the reference model.
Artifacts: **Semantic Mapping Strategy** (CIM↔PIM roles, figures linking OntoUML concepts to gUFO classes and hriv mappings).
Impact: faster reviews with domain experts; safer reasoning; clearer evidence when justifying mappings.
**Call to action:** If you design schemas/ontologies, adopt the two-layer approach: validate the OntoUML first, then encode in OWL—mapping external resources via hriv.
Refs: [Mapping strategy: CIM/PIM roles & visuals][4]; [Initiative home][1]. (Accessed 6 Nov 2025)

## Post 2: Non-intrusive by Design – Works with What You Have

What if you could add interoperability without altering a thing? That's the idea behind HRI-SI's non-intrusive approach.

No invasive changes, no system overhauls – just an added layer of meaning on top.
The payoff? You get interoperability as a **drop-in upgrade**, not a costly rebuild.
Your current standards and databases remain intact, now augmented with clear, shareable semantics.
**Plug in** our mappings and keep moving.

## Post 3: Your Definition, Connected – Plural Meanings, Shared Understanding

Different teams use different definitions – can they still work together? In HRI-SI, **pluralism** is a feature, not a bug.
We don't enforce one "true" vocabulary. Instead, we embrace multiple perspectives and map them to a common understanding.
It's common for systems to use the same term for different concepts, or different terms for the same concept, causing ambiguity and inconsistency.
Our approach resolves these collisions by linking each local definition to a shared reference ontology.
For example, if an external model defines `ont:Human`, we map it to our `hrio:Person` via **`hriv:hasExactMeaning`**, indicating that the external term carries the same meaning as our reference concept.
Your context-specific terminology retains its identity, but now everyone can see its semantic equivalent.
This mapping strategy preserves your intent while eliminating misinterpretation across systems.
You keep *your* definitions; HRI-SI just connects them.
**Coexist, then connect** – contribute your own mappings or tell us where you face semantic disconnects, and let's solve them together **🔗** [4][5]

## Post 4: Depth over Sprawl – Focus on High-Leverage Concepts

Trying to model *everything* means understanding nothing.
HRI-SI chooses depth over sprawl: we focus on the vital few concepts that truly move the needle.
Rather than a superficial model of all healthcare, we prioritize **high-impact, cross-cutting concepts** that many standards share.
For instance, we've zeroed in on core notions like person identity, sex and gender, and health conditions – concepts that appear across standards such as HL7 FHIR and OMOP.
And we model each chosen area with rigor.
Our ontology's **Sex and Gender** module, for example, provides a clear, precise and inclusive conceptual framework distinguishing intrinsic properties (like chromosomal sex) from extrinsic ones (like legal gender).
We won't try to boil the ocean; we'll deeply align the key domains that deliver the most interoperability bang for the buck.
The result is a lean ontology with **strong semantics** where it matters most.
Not everything, just *what matters*.
Have a critical concept causing integration headaches? Chances are we're on it – join the conversation or suggest the next high-leverage concept to tackle **🔗** [1][6]

Heard that semantic modeling is "too academic"? Think again.
HRI-SI is grounded in research *and* built for real-world impact.
